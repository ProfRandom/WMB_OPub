# The Main Sequence
 ![[Reverse H-R Diagram-Expanded.png]]

> This diagram is horizontally reversed from traditional H-R diagrams, which have hotter temperatures to the left and cooler temperatures to the right.  There are historical reasons for the “backward” format of traditional H–R diagrams, but for Wimby purposes, a diagram that follows intuitive left–right and up–down conventions is more useful.  This diagram contains the same information as a standard H–R diagram, presented in a more intuitive orientation.

In stellar astronomy, the **Main Sequence** refers to the long-lived phase of a star’s life during which it fuses hydrogen into helium in its core. During this phase, a star maintains hydrostatic equilibrium, and its luminosity, surface temperature, radius, and lifetime are tightly correlated.

Most stars spend the majority of their lifetimes on the Main Sequence, and many of the [[01. Stars and Spectral Classes|empirical relations]] used in stellar astrophysics are derived from stars in this phase.

The concept emerged alongside the development of the [Hertzsprung–Russell diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertzsprung–Russell_diagram) (H-R diagram) in the early 20th century, as astronomers recognized that stars cluster along a distinct band when plotted by luminosity and temperature.

In **Wimby stellamonics**, the Main Sequence is treated not merely as a classification, but as a **stable stellar regime** — the domain in which long-term orbital structure, energy availability, and habitability can be meaningfully discussed.  This is the regime in which the [[05. stellamon Equations of State|power-law relations]] used throughout stellamonics are valid to first order.

Unless otherwise stated, Wimby stellamonic relations assume a star lies on the Main Sequence. This assumption is not universal, but it is explicit.

## Pre- and Post-Main Sequence

A pre-[Main-Sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_sequence) star is:

- a collapsing protostar    
- powered primarily by **gravitational contraction**, not fusion    
- still settling toward hydrostatic and thermal equilibrium

Pre-Main-Sequence stars are short-lived as a phase (they evolve into Main Sequence stars rapidly), unstable, and poor hosts for long-term planetary habitability.

This phase ends when:

- the core becomes hot and dense enough    
- **sustained hydrogen fusion** begins    
- the star enters the Main Sequence

> [!important]
> There is no category of hydrogen-fusing star that skips the Main Sequence because it’s too massive.

[UY Scuti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UY_Scuti) ($909$ times the Sun's radius and $124,000$ times its luminosity), for instance, began its life as a very massive Main Sequence star.  It has since evolved _off_ the Main Sequence into a late-stage supergiant.

- it started as a very massive [[01. Stars and Spectral Classes|O- or early B-type]] star    
- it burned hydrogen in its core (Main Sequence phase)    
- it exhausted that hydrogen very quickly (a few million years)
- it expanded enormously as fusion moved into shells    
- it is now in a **short, unstable, late evolutionary phase**

UY Scuti is an extreme example of a post-Main Sequence star.

UY Scuti also illustrates a common source of confusion in stellar classification — and a frequent embarrassment in worldmaking.  Spectral class describes a star’s [**surface temperature**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_temperature) (or *effective temperature*, sometimes denoted $T_{eff}$), _not_ its size, mass, luminosity, **or** evolutionary state.  While Main Sequence stars of a given spectral class tend to share broadly similar properties, this correspondence breaks down completely once a star leaves the Main Sequence.

UY Scuti is [[01. Stars and Spectral Classes|Spectral Class M]] (M2 – M4); however, it is not an "M-dwarf", but a hypergiant of [[02. Stellar Luminosity Classes|luminosity class 0]].  The reason it shares a spectral class with small red stars like [Proxima Centauri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxima_Centauri) (M5.5) is that spectral classes and spectral types are based on a star's surface (effective) temperature.  UY Scuti has a surface temperature of $3500$ K compared to Proxima Centauri's $2992$ K, so they are similar in temperature and color, but that is where any similarities end.  This is why luminosity classes were invented ([[02. Stellar Luminosity Classes]]).

So, a post–Main Sequence star may share a spectral class with the Sun or other familiar stars while differing from them by orders of magnitude in radius, luminosity, and energy output. For this reason, stellamonics treats spectral class as **necessary but not sufficient** for understanding a star’s structural and habitability implications.

## Post–Main Sequence Stars

Once a star exhausts the hydrogen fuel in its core, it leaves the Main Sequence. From this point onward, the tight parameter coupling that characterizes Main Sequence stars begins to break down.

Luminosity, radius, surface temperature, and energy output may change rapidly or non-monotonically, often on timescales short compared to planetary climate or biological evolution.

For this reason, post–Main Sequence stars fall outside the default assumptions of stellamonics. They are not [[13. Habitable Zones of Giant Stars|ignored]], but they are treated as special cases rather than baseline environments.
