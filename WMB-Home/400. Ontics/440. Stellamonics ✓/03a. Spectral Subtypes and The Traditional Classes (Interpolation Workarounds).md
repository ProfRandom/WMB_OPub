# Spectral Subtypes and The Traditional Classes (Interpolation Workarounds)

If you want to assign subtypes within the traditional spectral class system, they can be **simulated** by [[11. Range Interpolation|numeric interpolation]].

## The Process

In the traditional system an F3-type star is assigned a temperature of $6750 $K and an F2-type star a temperature of $6820$ K.  Assuming you want your star to have a temperature of $6790$ K (because that temperature gives you a stellar mass, luminosity, etc. that fits your needs), that means that your star traditionally "falls somewhere between F2 and F3".  In the traditional system, that's really all you can say about it.

But, if you want to be more ... exacting ... here's a workaround: use numeric interpolation.

### The Equation

The equation form is simple;

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{subtype} &= n + \frac{x - min}{max - min} \\
\end{aligned}
$$

Where:
- $x$ = your star's temperature in Kelvin (in this case: 6790 K)
- $min$ = the lower end of the temperature range (in this case: 6750 K)
- $max$ = the upper end of the temperature range (in this case: 6820 K)
- $n$ = the number of the spectral type of the *lower temperature* class (in this case: 2)

Substituting in the appropriate values:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{subtype} &= 2 + \frac{6790 - 6750}{6820 - 6750} \\[1ex]
&= 2 + \frac{40}{70} \\[1ex]
&= 2\; + 0.571\cdots \\[1ex]
\text{subtype} &= \boxed{2.571} 
\end{aligned}
$$

## Caveats
- This assumes that there is a linear temperature gradient between:

	- spectral types within a spectral class, and
	- spectral spectral classes

 **Neither of these assumptions is canonically true.**

Traditionally, _there is no temperature gradient between F2- and F3-type stars._ 

Even if there were, it may follow a power law instead of a linear function.

Similarly, the traditional temperature of an A0-type star is $9700$ K, and that of a B9-type star is $10700$ K (those nice, even, round numbers should make you suspicious, for a start).

If your chosen star has a temperature of $9925$ K (which is completely legitimate, of course), the traditional system would simply classify it as an A0-type star.  If you want to be more specific, follow the above system:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{subtype} &= 0 + \frac{9925 - 9700}{10700 - 9700} \\[1ex]
&= 0 + \frac{225}{1000} \\[1ex]
&= 0\; + ≈0.225 \\[1ex]
&= \boxed{0.225} 
\end{aligned}
$$

 — and you can _call_ your star an A0.225 type star.
 
 But this designation is **not astronomically valid** and exists *solely as a modeling convenience.*

This method produces a numerically tidy label — not a physically meaningful one.
