# Introduction

For WMB modeling purposes, luminosity class is actually **more consequential** than spectral class. It determines whether [[01. Stars and Spectral Classes|main-sequence scaling relations]] apply — or fail catastrophically.  Spectral classification remains descriptive, while luminosity class defines the star’s structural regime.
## History

[[01. Stars and Spectral Classes|Spectral classes]] came first starting in the late 19th and early 20th centuries at [Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_classification) and the _observational_ work of:

- [Annie Jump Cannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annie_Jump_Cannon)
- [Antonia Maury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonia_Maury)
- [Williamina Fleming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Williamina_Fleming)

 — under project director [Edward C. Pickering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Charles_Pickering)

Stars were organized and grouped by strength and pattern of absorption lines in their spectra, and the sequence began very prosaically with A, B, C,....  At first, surface temperature ($\Theta, T_{eff}$) was not considered.

By 1910, Annie Jump Cannon had reorganized what was becoming a chaotic hodgepodge into the now familiar O, B, A, F, G, K, M order, adding subtypes 0 – 9 to refine the system.

It was [Ceclia Payne-Gaposchkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecilia_Payne-Gaposchkin) who demonstrated that spectral differences are primarily driven by temperature.  Importantly: The spectral classes were _not redesigned_ after this discovery — they were **reinterpreted**, and this is where the irregular temperature spans discussed in [[03. The Shortcomings of the Traditional Spectral Scale]].

In the 1940s that [William W. Morgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Wilson_Morgan), [Philip C. Keenan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Childs_Keenan), and [Edith Kellman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edith_Kellman) examined:

- Surface gravity
- _Width_ of absorption lines, and
- pressure-sensitive spectral features

 — adding a second axis to the existing Harvard classification.  In 1943, Morgan and Keenan — building on Kellman’s earlier work — published their landmark paper: *An Atlas of Stellar Spectra* (1943) establishing **luminosity classes** as a core component of stellar classification.
 
## Luminosity Classes (Structure / Evolutionary Stage)

Whereas spectral class focuses on a star's absorption lines and surface temperature, luminosity classes focus on **what the star *does* — by size, brightness, and evolutionary state**.  The modern luminosity classes are:

- 0 = Hypergiant 
- Ia/Iab/Ib = Supergiant 
- II = Bright giant 
- III = Giant 
- IV = Subgiant 
- V = Main sequence (dwarf) 
- VI = Subdwarf 
- VII = White dwarf

Within WMB, an **Evolutionary Phase** descriptor may be applied, such as:

 - Protostar
 - Main sequence
 - Giant
 - White dwarf
 - Remnant (NS/BH)
## 🔄 Intersections

- **Spectral Type** = Intersection of **Spectral Class/Subclass** with **Luminosity Class** 

Examples:
- the Sun = **G2V** (Spectral Class G, subclass 2, Luminosity Class V). 
- Proxima Centauri = **M5.5V**. 
- Rigel = **B8Ia**. 

## 🧭 Principle

- **Spectral Class = *surface physics* (temperature, color, composition). 
- **Luminosity Class = *structural physics* (radius, brightness, lifecycle stage).

Both are **lateral axes**; their intersection defines the star’s **Spectral Type**, as depicted in the [[Reverse H-R Diagram-Expanded.png|H-R Diagram]].

Examples:
- Sun = G (Class) → G2 (Subclass) → Luminosity V → **G2V Spectral Type**. 
- Betelgeuse = M (Class) → M2 (Subclass) → Luminosity Ia → **M2Ia Spectral Type**.

> [!important]
> Some confusion may arise here: colloquially, the number attached to a spectral class is often referred to as representing the spectral _type_ (and WMB follows this convention), but technically the number is the **subtype** and the spectral **type** is the **entire designation**: class + number + luminosity class.

### What the MK Luminosity Classes Actually Fixed

Before MK:

- A G-type dwarf and a G-type giant could (and still do) share the same spectral class 
- Their luminosities, radii, and evolutionary states were wildly different    
- Any attempt at modeling returned nonsense

MK solved this by separating:

- **temperature regime** (spectral class)    
- **structural regime** (luminosity class)    

That’s the moment when stellar classification became **operationally useful**, not just descriptive.

# Summary

Spectral classes emerged as an observational sorting scheme around 1900 and were later interpreted as a temperature sequence. The Morgan–Keenan system, introduced in the 1940s, added luminosity classes to distinguish structural regimes, making stellar classification physically and computationally meaningful.

Spectral class tells you what a star looks like; luminosity class tells you what physics applies.
